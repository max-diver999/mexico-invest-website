Quick answer: San Felipe is the cheapest established coastal market in this corpus, on the Sea of Cortez about two and a half hours south of the Mexicali border crossing. Indicative asking bands run about $90,000 to $170,000 in town and $170,000 to $300,000 for beachfront or beach view. The two facts that shape it are an extreme tidal range and a history of ejido title problems that demand a full registry check.

This is the market that shows what Mexican coastal property looked like before the resort corridor. A malecon, a shrimp fleet, RV parks, a few hundred foreign residents, and prices that have not been rewritten by international demand.

That is the appeal and it is also, precisely, the risk profile.

---

## Where people buy and what it costs

The town is small enough that the market divides by distance to the water and by whether a development has services. September 2026 asking prices sit behind this table, and none of them is a valuation.

| Zone | Indicative band | Character | Note |
|---|---:|---|---|
| Town and malecon | $90,000 to $170,000 | Walkable, restaurants, the shrimp fleet | Older stock, mixed services |
| Beachfront north of town | $180,000 to $320,000 | Houses on the sand, wide tidal flats | Verify the title chain twice |
| El Dorado Ranch area | $120,000 to $260,000 | Large long-standing development, amenities | Community fees, check what they cover |
| South toward Puertecitos | $110,000 to $250,000 | Remote, quieter, thinner services | Power and water arrangements vary |
| Inland lots | $30,000 to $90,000 | Bare land | Highest title risk in the region |
| Gated communities | $200,000 to $400,000 | Managed, security, pools | Check the reserve and occupancy |

The gap between the inland lot prices and everything else is where foreign buyers have historically lost money here. Cheap land with a convincing contract is the recurring pattern on this coast, and the cost of checking it properly is a small fraction of the cost of getting it wrong.

## The drive, and why distance kept it small

| Origin | Distance | Typical drive |
|---|---:|---|
| Mexicali and Calexico crossing | About 120 miles | 2 to 2.5 hours on Highway 5 |
| San Diego | About 235 miles | Around 4 hours via Mexicali |
| Phoenix | About 390 miles | 6 to 7 hours |
| Ensenada, over the sierra | About 150 miles | 3 hours, scenic and slower |

Two and a half hours from a border is a different market from forty minutes. It filters out the casual weekender entirely and leaves people who come for a week, a season or permanently. That is why San Felipe has an RV and snowbird culture rather than a Friday night one, and why its prices never took the step change that Rosarito's did.

A further point that rarely appears in listings concerns Highway 5 itself. The road from Mexicali was substantially rebuilt and the southern section toward Puertecitos and on to Highway 1 was completed in recent years, which changed San Felipe from a dead end into a through route on the peninsula. That has slowly widened the town's catchment beyond Mexicali and Calexico, and it is worth understanding as a structural change to access rather than as a marketing claim about future growth.

**Insider tip:** ask what the property's arrangement is for electricity and water specifically, not whether it "has" them. On the edges of this town and south toward Puertecitos, some properties run on solar with battery storage and delivered water, which is entirely workable but is a different ownership commitment from a municipal connection.

## The tide, and what it does to beachfront

Near the head of the Sea of Cortez the gulf narrows and shallows, and the tidal range grows to among the largest anywhere. At San Felipe the difference between high and low water can approach six metres on spring tides.

The practical effects are concrete. At low water the sea withdraws far enough that the beach becomes a broad expanse of firm seabed, walkable for a long way out. At high water it returns to the seawall. Boats are launched and recovered on the tide table rather than on a whim, and the local fleet works to it.

For a buyer three things follow. A listing photograph is a snapshot of one tidal state and may not represent the view most of the day. Any structure near the water has a maintenance history worth reading carefully. And a renter who arrived expecting to swim on demand may be disappointed, which shows up in reviews rather than in the brochure.

## Title, and the local history behind the warning

San Felipe deserves a more explicit warning about title than most markets, because the region has a documented history of disputes involving land that was sold to foreign buyers without the agrarian conversion ever completing.

The mechanism is always the same. Ejido land is held communally under agrarian law. It cannot pass to a private buyer until converted through the dominio pleno process and registered as private property. Where that conversion never happened, the buyer holds paperwork that does not stand against the registry, and possession can be challenged years later by parties with a genuine agrarian claim.

The checks are not difficult and they are all remote. Pull the registry folio and confirm the seller is the registered owner. Where ejido appears anywhere in the chain, require the conversion resolution on record rather than described. Obtain a certificado de libertad de gravamen dated within 30 days. Survey the boundaries against the registry plan. Use an attorney you appointed, not one the seller recommended.

The full mechanism is set out in [ejido land risks in Mexico](/guides/ejido-land-risks-mexico/).

## Pros and cons

**Pros.** The lowest coastal entry price of any market in this corpus, with beachfront achievable under $300,000. A long-established foreign community with the services that implies. Genuine quiet, which is the point for most buyers here. No flight required from the southwestern United States. Fishing and boating culture with real depth.

**Cons.** Summer heat above 40 degrees that empties the town for months. A small resale market that can take a long time to clear at higher price points. Ejido title history that raises the standard of diligence on every parcel. Services that vary property by property on the edges of town. Very limited nightly rental demand outside the season.

## Which buyer this suits

**A snowbird or retiree wanting quiet at the lowest coastal price.** This is the market's core and has been for decades.

**A boating or fishing buyer.** The Sea of Cortez and the local fleet culture are the genuine draw, and the tide is part of the craft rather than an obstacle.

**A buyer with time to do title properly.** The prices here reward patience with paperwork more than any other market on this site.

It suits a yield investor least, because the rental season is short and the market thin.
