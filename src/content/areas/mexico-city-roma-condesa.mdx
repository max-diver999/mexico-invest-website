Most foreign capital entering Mexico stops at the coast, which is why these two colonias are consistently under-analysed relative to their size. They sit inside a metropolitan area of roughly 22 million people, carry the deepest long-term tenant pool in the country, and are governed by an ownership regime that coastal buyers spend thousands of dollars working around. Before you compare them with a beach purchase, read [the restricted zone explained](/guides/mexico-restricted-zone-explained/) — the geography is the whole reason the economics differ.

## Why does Mexico City ownership work differently from the coast?

Article 27 restricts direct foreign ownership only within 50 km of a coastline and 100 km of an international border, and Mexico City sits roughly 300 km from either ocean. Roma Norte and Condesa therefore require no fideicomiso, no SRE permit and no annual trust fee, sparing foreign buyers about $2,700-5,000 of setup at closing.

The practical consequence is a shorter, cheaper transaction. A coastal buyer applies to the Secretaría de Relaciones Exteriores for a permit, pays the MXN 21,650 federal duty, selects a trust bank, and carries an annual fee for the next fifty years. A Roma Norte buyer signs an escritura in their own name at a notaría and is finished.

| Cost line | Roma Norte / Condesa | Playa del Carmen equivalent |
|---|---:|---:|
| SRE permit (federal duty) | Not applicable | $1,200-1,700 |
| Trust establishment | Not applicable | $500-1,500 |
| Trust legal drafting | Not applicable | $500-800 |
| Annual trust fee, per year | Not applicable | $500-800 |
| Acquisition tax (ISAI) | 3-4.5% progressive | 2-3% |
| Ten-year trust carry | $0 | $5,000-8,000 |

The acquisition tax runs higher in Mexico City than in Quintana Roo, so the saving is not total. On a $223,000 purchase the CDMX ISAI advantage is negative by roughly $2,500, while the avoided trust costs are worth $2,700-5,000 at closing and a further $5,000-8,000 across a ten-year hold. Direct title also simplifies inheritance, which a substitute beneficiary clause handles adequately but not identically.

## What do the two colonias actually cost?

Roma Norte trades at $3,200-4,800 USD per square metre in 2026 and Condesa ranges from $3,400 to $5,200, while Roma Sur costs about 20-25% less at $2,600-3,600. Pricing in this market tracks building era and street rather than finish level, which is the opposite of how coastal inventory is valued and the reason two apparently similar units differ by 40%.

| Colonia | USD per sqm | 62 sqm 1BR | 90 sqm 2BR | Character |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---|
| Condesa | $3,400-5,200 | $211K-322K | $306K-468K | Park frontage, art deco |
| Roma Norte | $3,200-4,800 | $198K-298K | $288K-432K | Restaurants, galleries, co-working |
| Juárez | $2,800-4,000 | $174K-248K | $252K-360K | Transitional, Reforma-adjacent |
| Roma Sur | $2,600-3,600 | $161K-223K | $234K-324K | Quieter, value entry |

Pricing in both colonias is set by scarcity of a specific building type rather than by square metres alone. Roma Norte and Condesa were largely built out between 1920 and 1940, which caps the supply of original art deco and Porfirian stock permanently, and the 2017 losses reduced it further. New construction on infill sites clears at $4,500-5,800 per square metre because it carries current-code engineering, while unreinforced 1960s slab buildings on the same street trade 30-40% below the colonia average and are difficult to finance or resell. Building era does most of the work, but three further attributes decide where a unit lands inside its band:

- **Original 1920s-1930s detail** — parquet, mouldings, iron balconies — carries a 10-15% premium because it cannot be reproduced
- **A working lift** adds roughly 8-12% and widens the tenant pool; a fourth-floor walk-up loses it
- **A structurally certified building** commands a premium that has widened every year since 2017, currently 15-25% over uncertified stock of the same era

 The escritura and the licencia de construcción settle the third point between them, and the next section explains why that premium is rational rather than sentimental.

## The seismic question, and why it outranks everything else

Mexico City's building code classifies ground by seismic behaviour, and Roma and Condesa sit almost entirely in Zone III, the soft Lake Texcoco clay that amplifies wave motion rather than damping it. For foreign buyers this classification requires an engineering answer before a price is agreed, and the review costs MXN 15,000-35,000, which is under 1% of a $223,000 apartment.

The amplification is not theoretical. The September 1985 earthquake concentrated its destruction in exactly this soil band, and the M7.1 event of 19 September 2017 brought down buildings across Roma, Condesa and Del Valle while leaving firmer-ground colonias a few kilometres west largely intact. Construction era is what separates one outcome from the other.

| Construction era | Governing code | Risk posture |
|---|---|---|
| Pre-1957 | No modern seismic code | Varies wildly; some perform well, some fail |
| 1957-1985 | Early code, widely under-enforced | Highest concentration of 2017 failures |
| 1987-2004 | Post-1985 code | Materially better, pre-modern detailing |
| 2004-2017 | NTC 2004 | Good, verify soil study on file |
| Post-2017 | NTC 2017/2020 | Current standard, strongest stock |

Four documents settle the question, and a seller who cannot produce them is telling you something. Work this checklist in order:

- **Constancia de seguridad estructural** — the safety certificate CDMX requires for defined building classes, renewed on a 5-year cycle
- **Licencia de construcción** — establishes the real permit year, not the year the listing claims
- **Post-September-2017 assessment** — what was inspected, what was found, what was repaired
- **DRO sign-off on any retrofit** — the Director Responsable de Obra carries personal legal responsibility for that certification
 Ask for the constancia de seguridad estructural, the certificate of structural safety that Mexico City requires for defined building classes and renews on a 5-year cycle. Ask for the year of the licencia de construcción, not the year the listing claims. Ask whether the building was assessed after September 2017 and what the report concluded. And where any retrofit was done, ask for the DRO sign-off: the Director Responsable de Obra is the licensed professional carrying personal responsibility for that certification, and a full structural file typically runs 30-60 pages.

Soil classification is the variable that makes this market unlike anywhere else covered on this site. Mexico City's Reglamento de Construcciones divides the valley into three seismic zones, and Roma and Condesa sit almost wholly in Zone III, the drained Lake Texcoco clay where ground motion is amplified rather than absorbed. A building of identical design performs measurably worse here than in Zone I, and the 19 September 2017 event concentrated roughly 40% of its recorded collapses in this soil band while firmer-ground colonias a few kilometres west stood. An engineering review costs MXN 15,000-35,000, under 1% of a $223,000 purchase. Construction era, not architectural style or asking price, is what separates the stock that behaved well from the stock that did not.

Budget MXN 15,000-35,000 for an independent structural engineer. On a $223,000 purchase that is well under 1% of the price, and it buys the one answer that decides whether the building is an asset or a liability. ## Rental economics: a worked example

Start with the unit foreign buyers most often consider: a 62 square metre one-bedroom in Roma Norte at $3,600 per square metre, or $223,000. Closing at 6.5% lifts the all-in basis to $237,500. Unfurnished it lets for about MXN 26,000 a month, or $17,148 a year at the Banxico reference rate, which is 7.2% gross and nets 4.5%.

| Line | Annual USD |
|---|---:|
| Gross rent, MXN 26,000/month | $17,148 |
| Building maintenance, MXN 2,800/month | −$1,846 |
| Predial (Mexico City) | −$550 |
| Landlord insurance | −$450 |
| Management at 8% | −$1,372 |
| Vacancy reserve, one month | −$1,429 |
| Repairs reserve at 5% | −$857 |
| Net operating income | $10,644 |

That NOI is 4.5% net on the $237,500 all-in basis. The gross figure sits above the 5.5-7% band this site uses for coastal short-term rentals, and the reason is structural rather than optimistic: Mexico City entry prices are low relative to achievable rent, and long-term letting avoids the 25-35% management load, the lodging tax and the turnover cost that compress beach yields. Compare the mechanics in the [rental yield guide](/guides/mexico-rental-yield-guide/) before assuming the two are interchangeable.

Where the coastal comparison actually breaks, per the operating lines above:

- No lodging tax: Quintana Roo charges 5% ISH on accommodation; a CDMX annual lease pays none
- Management at 8% rather than 25-35%, because an annual tenancy needs no guest operation
- No furnishing capex of $15,000-30,000 and no year-two refresh
- Vacancy of one month rather than the 35-45% of nights a nightly-let calendar leaves empty

Predial deserves a note of its own. Mexico City assesses property tax on cadastral value at rates that leave most Roma and Condesa apartments paying between MXN 6,000 and MXN 20,000 a year — a fraction of what an equivalent US or Canadian property carries, and low enough that it rarely changes a decision.

## What changed for short-term letting?

Mexico City approved a 2024 reform that caps hosting on a single dwelling at roughly 180 nights a year and requires registration in a hosting padrón, after sustained pressure over housing costs in exactly these colonias. The measure is contested and has been revised since, so foreign buyers should confirm the operative night count with the borough before underwriting any nightly income at all.

Two checks matter more than the city rule. The building's own reglamento de condominio frequently prohibits stays under 30 days regardless of what the city permits, and that restriction binds you directly. And the tenant economics rarely justify the friction: a furnished 30-day-plus corporate or nomad let in Roma Norte achieves MXN 32,000-45,000 a month with none of the registry exposure, which lands close to nightly-rate revenue after the platform, cleaning and vacancy costs come out.

| Letting model | Typical monthly revenue | Regulatory exposure |
|---|---:|---|
| Nightly, under 30 days | MXN 38,000-55,000 gross | City night cap, hosting padrón, building reglamento |
| Furnished 1-6 months | MXN 32,000-45,000 | Outside the short-stay rule; RFC and SAT filing apply |
| Unfurnished annual | MXN 24,000-30,000 | Lowest; standard lease and predial only |

The red flag to watch is a listing that quotes nightly revenue without naming the building's reglamento de condominio, because that document binds you regardless of what the city permits and typically takes 2-3 days to obtain.

The practical position for most foreign buyers is a furnished medium-term let of one to six months. It sits outside the short-stay restriction, serves the remote-worker demand the area actually generates, and produces a higher net than either pure nightly letting or unfurnished annual tenancy.

## Pros and cons for investors

The trade-off in this market is structural rather than marginal: a Mexico City purchase costs roughly 1% more in ISAI acquisition tax than a Quintana Roo equivalent, and returns that in avoided fideicomiso costs worth $2,700-5,000 at closing plus $500-800 every year of the hold. The table below sets both columns against each other.

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Direct fee-simple title, no trust, no annual fee | Higher acquisition tax than Quintana Roo, 3-4.5% |
| Deepest long-term tenant pool in Mexico | Zone III seismic soil demands real engineering diligence |
| 7% gross on long lets, above coastal STR corridors | Short-term letting restricted by city rule and building bylaws |
| Year-round demand, no hurricane season | Air quality and traffic materially worse than coastal markets |
| Peso rent from a domestic tenant base | Peso rent against a USD purchase creates currency mismatch |
| Resale market driven by residents, not tourism cycles | Gentrification backlash is a live political risk |

The currency point cuts both ways and deserves precision. A coastal short-term rental earns dollars from foreign guests against peso costs, which favours the owner when the peso weakens. A Roma Norte long-let earns pesos from Mexican and resident tenants against a dollar-denominated purchase, which does the opposite. Model both, and read the [currency risk guide](/guides/currency-risk-mexico-property-usd/) before assuming your coastal instincts transfer.

## Who should buy in Roma Norte or Condesa?

Three buyer profiles account for most successful purchases in this market, and each requires a different entry price: roughly $175,000-225,000 for a yield-focused Roma Sur unit, $223,000 for a Roma Norte one-bedroom on an annual tenancy, and $306,000 or more for the park-frontage Condesa stock that foreign buyers acquire for capital preservation rather than income.

**The yield-focused diversifier.** A buyer holding coastal Mexican property who wants uncorrelated income adds a Roma Sur or Juárez one-bedroom at $175,000-225,000, lets it unfurnished on annual tenancies, and accepts 4.5% net with almost no operational burden. Filing is a single annual ISR return with an RFC, and predial runs MXN 6,000-20,000 a year. This is the strongest fit on the page.

**The remote worker turning resident.** Someone already spending months a year in Condesa buys rather than rents, occupies part of the year, and runs 30-day-plus furnished lets in between. Direct title makes this materially simpler than the coastal equivalent, and the residency question is covered in the [temporary resident visa guide](/guides/temporary-resident-visa-buy-property-mexico/).

**The capital-preservation buyer.** A purchaser prioritising a defensible asset over yield takes a structurally certified Condesa building on Parque México, accepts roughly 4% net, and holds on the reasoning that park-frontage stock in a supply-constrained colonia is the last thing to fall in a soft market.

Matching profile to product, at a glance:

- Yield-focused diversifier → Roma Sur or Juárez 1BR, $175,000-225,000, unfurnished annual tenancy, 4.5% net
- Remote worker turning resident → Condesa or Roma Norte 1BR, $223,000-298,000, part-year occupancy plus 30-day-plus lets
- Capital-preservation buyer → Condesa park frontage, $306,000+, structurally certified, roughly 4% net

**The nightly-rental maximiser should not buy here.** The city rule, the building bylaws and the tenant mix all work against it, and the same capital in Playa del Carmen or Puerto Vallarta faces none of those obstacles.

## What red flags should pause a Roma or Condesa purchase?

Six patterns recur often enough in Roma and Condesa to be worth naming, and half of them exist only because of Zone III. Every one of them is cheaper to find than to fix. An independent structural engineer charges MXN 15,000-35,000 to look, and the least expensive of these defects costs several multiples of that to remedy once the escritura is signed.

- No constancia de seguridad estructural, or a seller who treats the request as unusual
- A building visibly repaired after 2017 with no DRO documentation of the work
- A listing describing 1970s construction as "classic" without naming the year
- Reglamento de condominio that bans stays under 30 days when the pitch assumes nightly income
- Predial receipts missing or showing arrears, which attach to the property rather than the seller
- Rent quoted in dollars, which signals a listing aimed at foreigners rather than the real tenant market

## What should you verify before committing?

Run these eight checks cheapest first and stop at the first one that fails rather than negotiating past it. Structural evidence leads deliberately, because a Zone III building with no engineering record is the one defect foreign buyers cannot price around, and verification requires MXN 15,000-35,000 and about 10 business days.

1. Establish the construction year from the licencia de construcción held by the alcaldía. Listing years in Roma and Condesa are frequently the renovation date rather than the build.
2. Obtain the constancia de seguridad estructural and any post-2017 assessment.
3. Commission an independent structural engineer, MXN 15,000-35,000.
4. Pull the escritura chain and a certificado de libertad de gravamen from the Registro Público de la Propiedad, issued within the last 30 days.
5. Read the reglamento de condominio for letting restrictions and pet, noise and works rules.
6. Request twelve months of maintenance receipts and the reserve fund balance.
7. Obtain a constancia de no adeudo for predial and check the water account separately, since Mexico City bills the two apart and arrears follow the property.
8. Price comparable rents on the same street, in pesos, from actual listings rather than from the seller.

The structural items sit first deliberately. Title defects in Mexico City are comparatively rare and largely fixable; a Zone III building with no engineering record is neither.
