Quick answer: San Carlos is a small boating and diving town on the Sonoran coast of the Sea of Cortez, next to the port of Guaymas and about five to six hours south of the Arizona border. Indicative asking bands run about $130,000 to $250,000 inland, $250,000 to $450,000 with a marina or bay view, and $450,000 upward for beachfront. It is the one drive-to market in this corridor that requires a vehicle import permit.

Most of Mexico's drive-to coastal markets are beach markets. San Carlos is a water market, which is a different thing: the assets that hold value here are the marina berths, the haul-out yard and the dive sites, not the sand.

That focus makes it smaller and steadier than its neighbours to the north.

---

## Where people buy and what it costs

The town wraps around the bay beneath the Tetakawi headland, and prices track the water view and the marina access. Asking levels here were sampled in September 2026 and move with the season.

| Zone | Indicative band | Character | Note |
|---|---:|---|---|
| Marina area | $250,000 to $450,000 | Condos and townhouses, berth access | Check berth ownership against tenancy |
| Bay-view hillside | $250,000 to $500,000 | Houses with the Tetakawi outlook | Access road grade varies |
| Beachfront, Los Algodones | $450,000 upward | Sand access, larger houses | Highest maintenance load |
| Town and inland | $130,000 to $250,000 | Residential, walkable to services | Where most year-round owners buy |
| Guaymas | $70,000 to $160,000 | Working port city, ten minutes away | Local tenants, not tourist rental |
| Gated developments | $300,000 to $600,000 | Pools, security, managed | Confirm the reserve and occupancy |

The marina question is the one buyers most often get wrong. A property advertised with marina access may carry an owned berth, a leased berth, or simply proximity to a marina where a berth must be rented separately at market rates. These are three different assets and the difference can run into tens of thousands of dollars over a holding period.

## The drive, and the permit that catches people out

| Origin | Distance | Typical drive |
|---|---:|---|
| Nogales crossing | About 260 miles | 4.5 to 5 hours on Highway 15 |
| Tucson | About 330 miles | 5.5 to 6 hours |
| Phoenix | About 440 miles | 7 to 8 hours |
| Puerto Penasco, along the coast | About 340 miles | 6 hours, inland via Caborca |

The permit point deserves emphasis because it is the single most common procedural error made by drivers who have previously only visited Baja or Rocky Point. Those destinations sit in free zones where no temporary vehicle import permit is needed. San Carlos does not. The permit is obtained at the border with the vehicle title, registration, passport and immigration form, against a refundable deposit that is returned when the permit is cancelled on exit.

One further local factor deserves a mention because it is invisible from a listing. San Carlos shares its municipal services and much of its labour force with Guaymas, a working port and industrial city ten minutes away. That proximity is why a town of this size supports trades, marine engineering and medical services at all, and it is also why the two places are frequently discussed as though they were one market when they behave as two. Property in Guaymas is priced for local tenants and local wages; property in San Carlos is priced for foreign buyers. Comparing the two on price per square metre without that context produces a misleading picture.

**Insider tip:** cancel the permit properly on the way out, every time, and keep the receipt. An uncancelled permit can block the issue of a new one on a later trip and can complicate selling the vehicle, and untangling it afterwards is far harder than the two minutes it takes at the booth.

## What the season actually looks like

San Carlos runs on a snowbird calendar rather than a holiday one, and the shape of the year is unusually pronounced.

From October to April the town fills with Canadian and American residents, many returning to the same property for the same months each year. Restaurants open, the marina is busy, and the dive operators run daily. From June to September temperatures climb well above 35 degrees, humidity rises, and a large share of the foreign population leaves. Some businesses close entirely for the summer.

For an owner this produces a clear and honest model: a season let of three to six months at a good monthly rate, or personal use across the same window, with the property closed or lightly used through summer. It does not produce a year-round nightly rental business, and any projection presenting one should be read carefully.

The corollary matters too. A property left empty through a Sonoran summer needs someone checking it. Air conditioning, pool systems, pumps and refrigeration all fail more often in that heat, and the difference between a caretaker arrangement and none is the difference between a small repair and a large one.

## Ownership costs

| Line | Indicative annual | Note |
|---|---:|---|
| Fideicomiso trustee | $500 to $800 | Every foreign purchase here |
| HOA in a gated development | $1,800 to $6,000 | Varies widely with amenities |
| Property insurance | $700 to $2,000 | Coastal Sonora, storm exposure |
| Predial property tax | $250 to $800 | Low by United States standards |
| Marina berth, if rented | Varies by length | Ask before assuming access |
| Caretaker through summer | $600 to $1,800 | Genuinely worth budgeting |

The caretaker line is the one that separates owners who have a good experience here from those who do not. It is a small number relative to the property and it addresses the specific risk this climate creates.

## Pros and cons

**Pros.** Genuine marine infrastructure that a town this size would not normally support, including full-service marinas and haul-out. Sheltered water and well-known dive sites. An established, returning snowbird community. Entry prices below the Pacific resort markets. A four-lane highway most of the way from the border.

**Cons.** A vehicle import permit requirement that catches out drivers used to Baja and Rocky Point. A hot, quiet summer with real business closures. A small market where higher-price resale can be slow. Rental economics built on season lets rather than nightly rates. Further from the border than any other market in this corridor.

---

## Which buyer this suits

**A boat owner or diver.** This is the whole logic of the market and no other drive-to Mexican town matches the marine infrastructure per resident.

**A snowbird wanting a returning winter base.** The season structure is well established and the community is used to it.

**A buyer who wants quiet over amenity.** San Carlos has never tried to become Rocky Point and shows no sign of trying now.

It suits a nightly rental investor least, and a buyer who wants year-round occupancy not at all.
